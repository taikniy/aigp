III.A / Lesson 01

Purpose before
prediction.

Before asking which model to build, decide whose problem you are solving—and what your AI must never decide.

Objective: define business context & use caseIII.A competency: 6–8 exam questionsCookbook: M6 L1 · p.164

By the end of this lesson

You should be able to turn a product idea into a bounded AI use case: a clear purpose, affected people, success criteria, decision authority and excluded uses.

Singapore case · hypothetical AI extension

“Which scheme can help me?”

A resident has recently lost their job. They want to know which support schemes might be relevant and what documents they need.

LifeSG brings citizen services together. Imagine adding a scheme-finder assistant that explains approved information in plain language. The product idea sounds useful—but “help residents” is too broad to govern.

This is an imagined feature for learning. It does not describe LifeSG’s actual AI implementation or promise any scheme’s availability or eligibility.

The first governance decision
is a product decision.

A use case describes the job a system is allowed to do in a particular setting. It connects a user need to the inputs, outputs and actions involved. Without that connection, teams can build a technically impressive model that performs the wrong task—or quietly takes on more authority than intended.

For our scheme-finder, the intended task is to help residents understand published information. Deciding whether someone qualifies for a benefit is a different task, with different consequences and controls.

The distinction to remember

“Explain a scheme’s published criteria” and “decide that a resident qualifies” are different use cases, even when they share the same chat interface.

1. Start with the problem, not the model.

Describe the friction people experience and the outcome you want to improve. Then consider whether AI is needed. A searchable guide or a clearer form may solve the problem with less complexity.

In our case: residents struggle to find and interpret scheme information. Compare an assistant with an improved search experience before choosing a language model.

2. Identify everyone the product affects.

The person typing is not the only stakeholder. Consider residents with limited digital literacy, people using different languages, frontline officers, policy owners and teams responsible for keeping information current.

In our case: a fluent answer can still exclude a resident who cannot use the interface. Accessibility and a usable officer handoff belong in the requirements.

3. Define the boundary and decision authority.

Specify permitted inputs, outputs and actions. State who makes consequential decisions and what happens when the system is uncertain. Human oversight needs an operational process, not merely a sentence saying that someone is “in the loop.”

In our case: the assistant explains approved guidance and links to official services. It cannot approve grants, invent eligibility criteria or submit an application without an authorised workflow.

4. Separate success from safeguards.

A business outcome tells you whether the product is useful. A model measure tells you how a component performs. A safeguard sets a boundary on acceptable operation. You need all three; none can substitute for the others.

In our case: task completion measures usefulness; evidence-grounded answer quality measures performance; blocking unsupported eligibility decisions limits harm.

A useful transfer: martech sentiment analysis

A brand may want to understand campaign reception from permitted public posts. Its model estimates sentiment in that sample. It does not establish what all Singapore residents think. Turning the same dashboard into a list of named critics would change the purpose, affected people and risks—and should trigger a fresh review.

How this connects to the rest of III.A

The use-case brief becomes an input to the impact assessment. Its boundaries guide architecture and oversight; its success criteria guide testing; its stakeholders guide consultation. Design decisions and accepted risks then become part of the documented record.

These are connected governance activities. Writing the brief alone does not complete impact assessment or prove that deployment is safe.

Worked artefact

A use-case brief you could
take into a product review.

Below is an illustrative brief for the scheme-finder. Notice how each choice gives designers, reviewers and operators something concrete to assess.

Hypothetical LifeSG extension · draft for learning
User problem
Residents cannot easily find relevant schemes or understand published application requirements.
Intended use
Explain current, approved scheme information and direct residents to official application or support channels.
Users & affected people
Residents, caregivers assisting them, frontline officers, scheme administrators, and people with language or accessibility needs.
Inputs
A resident’s question and an approved, versioned collection of scheme guidance. Do not request NRIC numbers or financial documents for this information-only task.
Outputs & authority
A source-linked explanation and suggested next steps. Scheme administrators retain eligibility and approval authority.
Excluded uses
No eligibility determination, approval, invented policy, independent application submission or training on residents’ conversations without separate assessment.
Success & evidence
Evaluate successful information-finding, answer fidelity to approved sources, accessibility and successful handoff. Agree thresholds and representative language tests before the pilot.
Controls & fallback
Use approved sources; show their dates; test unsupported advice; route uncertain cases to support. If sources are stale or unreliable, fall back to verified service links.
Ownership & change
A product owner owns scope; policy owners approve source updates; operations owns handoffs. Adding eligibility assessment requires a new use-case and risk review.

This is a teaching artefact, not an official agency policy. Thresholds must be justified for the actual product. Public-sector data governance requirements must be assessed in context; do not assume private-sector PDPA duties apply identically to public agencies.

One objective, three kinds of evidence.

UsefulCan residents find the information they need?
ReliableAre explanations faithful to approved sources?
BoundedDoes the assistant avoid decisions it is not authorised to make?
Avoid substituting one measure for another.
Weak requirementBetter requirement
“The model must be 95% accurate.”Define what counts as correct, the evaluation set, language coverage and the severity of different errors. Agree a defensible threshold.
“An officer will oversee it.”Specify when a case is handed off, which team receives it, what context they receive and who can suspend the feature.
“It must make residents happy.”Measure task completion and source fidelity as well as user satisfaction. A confident wrong answer may receive a positive rating.
What if the product changes?

The team proposes “automatic eligibility prediction” to reduce officer workload. Treat this as a changed use case—not a minor interface improvement. Reassess data needs, affected people, error consequences, decision authority and review mechanisms.

Try a different product: public communications

A communications assistant may draft announcements from approved sources, with an officer approving publication. Giving it permission to publish directly changes its authority. Red teaming should then test false policy statements, impersonation, prompt injection and misleading translations—but those tests support, rather than replace, a defined use case and approval process.

Recall & reason

Can you recognise
the governance decision?

Choose an answer before checking the explanation. These are original practice questions, not official IAPP questions.

1. A team has chosen a large language model for a citizen assistant. It has not agreed whether the assistant explains schemes or determines eligibility. What is the best next step?

B is best. The missing prerequisite is the business context and use-case boundary. Better latency, a disclaimer or a larger model cannot decide the product’s authorised purpose.

A pilot can provide evidence once its scope, risks and controls are defined; it should not substitute for those decisions.

2. A sentiment dashboard was approved to report aggregate campaign reactions. A manager now wants it to identify individual critics for sales staff to contact. What should happen first?

C is best. Individual targeting changes the approved use and potential harms. Public availability does not settle rights, acceptable reuse or governance requirements.

Better accuracy does not authorise a new purpose, and the model cannot assume the organisation’s accountability.

Explain it without looking.

  • What problem does the product solve?
  • Who is affected beyond the person using it?
  • Which decisions belong to the system, and which belong to people?
  • How do business outcomes, model measures and safeguards differ?
  • What change would trigger a new review?

The lesson in one sentence

A governable AI product starts with a clear purpose, a bounded role and evidence of usefulness and acceptable risk—not with a model choice.
